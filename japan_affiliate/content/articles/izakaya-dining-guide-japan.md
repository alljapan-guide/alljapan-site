JNTO describes the izakaya as a place for after-work dinner parties, and it is a convenient way for a visitor to try a range of dishes in one evening. It is also a place where the unwritten rules can feel confusing: nobody gives you a set menu, plates arrive in no fixed order, and the drinks are part of the point. The Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO) publishes an izakaya dining guide that explains the basics. This page follows that guide, adds a practical plan for a first visit, and labels our own opinion as "Our view", so sourced information and editorial advice stay separate.

## How to eat at an izakaya

The key habit is sharing. JNTO says that {{fact:izk_share}}. The practical detail is the plate: {{fact:izk_torizara}}.

```diagram

steps: Order several dishes for the table | Dishes arrive and each guest gets a torizara | Take your portion from the main plate onto your own plate | Keep ordering as you go, food and drinks
```

Our view: order two or three dishes first and add more after they arrive. A group of friends can reach a full meal this way without over-ordering on the first round.

## Dietary needs

An izakaya menu mixes meat, fish and seafood, and the dishes are meant to be shared, so it can be hard to avoid an ingredient. ## FAQ

### What is an izakaya?

JNTO describes an izakaya as a casual Japanese pub serving drinks and a variety of food. They are informal and good for sharing.

### How do you eat at an izakaya?

Food is meant to be shared. Each guest gets a torizara, a small plate, and takes a portion from the main plate.

### What should I order at an izakaya?

JNTO lists sashimi platters, karaage, yakisoba noodles and edamame as items available at most izakaya, plus hot pot in the colder months.

### Where can I find an izakaya?

JNTO says izakaya can be found near any major train station in Japan.

### Can I order without Japanese?

A chain such as Watami has a picture menu for easy ordering, according to JNTO. You can also point at dishes or ask the staff.
