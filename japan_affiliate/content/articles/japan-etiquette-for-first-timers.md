Most visitors worry about etiquette more than they need to. The Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO) takes a relaxed view: politeness and common sense go a long way, and when you are unsure, you can ask. This guide summarises the JNTO page on customs and manners and turns it into a short checklist.

Where we add advice, we label it "Our view", so you can tell JNTO's guidance from our opinion.

## Why the rules feel strict, and why you should not worry

JNTO explains that {{fact:cm_smooth}}. Our view: that is a helpful way to read the rules. They are not tests for visitors. They are the habits that keep crowded places calm, and a visitor who is quiet, patient and willing to ask is already doing most of what is needed. Mistakes are normal, and a polite apology and a smile usually settle them. If you want one habit to take with you, make it the one JNTO opens with: ask when you are unsure.

## A short checklist

- **Ask when unsure.** Staff will be happy to help.
- **Follow the locals.** It solves most questions.
- **Keep your phone quiet** on transport.
- **Do not tip** at most hotels.
- **Take off your shoes** where the entrance shows it is expected, as in a ryokan.
- **Ask about meals and baths** at a traditional inn.

## FAQ

### Do I have to bow in Japan?

No. JNTO says that, as a guest, you will more likely be bowed at than expected to bow. A small nod with a thank you is a nice extra gesture.

### Can I eat sushi with my hands?

Yes. JNTO says you can eat sushi with your hands.

### Is it rude to slurp noodles?

JNTO says local culture dictates that you slurp rather than chew noodles, though whether you slurp or not is up to you.

### Do I need to tip?

JNTO says that most western hotels in Japan follow the usual practices, with the exception of tipping, which is not needed.

### What should I do if I am unsure what is polite?

JNTO says to ask if you are unsure, and that if in doubt, you can just follow the locals.
