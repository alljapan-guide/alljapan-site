## What an IC card actually is

An IC card is a contactless prepaid card. You tap it on the reader at the gate, the fare is taken automatically when you exit, and you top it up with cash at a machine. It removes the hardest part of the first day in a Japanese station: working out the fare from a route map before you can buy a paper ticket.

{{fact:ic_ten_cards}}. That is the headline, but note the limit. PASMO's own page says {{fact:pasmo_not_every_line}}, so a rural line or a small bus company can still be the exception. When you plan a trip outside the big cities, check the local operator's page before you rely on the card.

## Suica vs Pasmo vs ICOCA: which one

{{tip|Do not spend time comparing them. Take whichever card is available at your arrival airport or station, or the one your phone can issue.}}

Our view: for a visitor the three names mean the same thing, so choose by what you can get easily. Which cards are sold to visitors, and where, is decided by the operators and changes over time; for example, the PASMO site {{fact:pasmo_tourist}}.

Always read the issuing operator's own page for the current rules, price and validity of the card you buy, because the terms are set by the operator and can change.

## How to use it on day one

At a ticket gate, hold the card flat against the reader until it beeps and the gate opens. On exit the gate takes the fare. On buses, tap at the reader by the door; the boarding and exit rules differ by bus type, so watch what the locals do the first time. At a convenience store, tell the cashier you will pay by IC card, then tap on the reader. Top-up methods differ by card and by phone, so follow the issuing operator's or Apple's instructions.

A sensible habit is to top up whenever the balance falls below the cost of a couple of rides, so you never meet a blocked gate during rush hour.

## When an IC card is not the best choice

An IC card pays standard fares. It is a convenience product, not a discount product.

- **Several long bullet train journeys in one week.** Compare the individual fares with a pass. Our [rail pass guide](is-japan-rail-pass-worth-it.html) shows how to do the sum, and you can check current options through {{aff:railpass|an authorised Japan Rail Pass seller}}.
- **Guided days out.** If you would rather have transport and a guide handled for you, bookable tours on {{aff:klook|Klook}} can replace several train legs with one booking.
- **Heavy sightseeing in one city.** Compare a day pass with your planned rides before you buy either.

## Common mistakes first-timers make

- **Letting the balance run low at a gate.** The gate will block you until you add value at a fare-adjustment or top-up machine.
- **Tapping with the wrong item.** If your wallet holds several cards, take out the one you want to use before you tap.
- **Loading too much value.** Check the operator's refund and expiry rules for your type of card before you load a large amount.
- **Assuming it works on every line.** Plan rural trips by checking the local operator.

## FAQ

### Can I use one IC card across different cities?

Yes, in the areas where the cards are accepted. The operators list ten interoperable cards; see the PASMO page in the Sources box for the list.

### What do I need to add a card to my iPhone?

Apple lists the requirements: {{fact:apple_wallet_needs}}. Read Apple's page for any further notes that apply to your region.

### Is an IC card cheaper than buying single tickets?

No. It pays standard fares. The benefit is speed and convenience.

### What happens if I lose the card?

The rules depend on the card type, so check the operator's page for the card you hold before you travel.

### Should I get a rail pass as well?

Only if the long-distance journeys you plan cost more than the pass. For city-based trips an IC card alone is usually enough.
