A tea ceremony is one of the quietest ways to spend an hour in Japan, and one of the easiest to get wrong if you arrive with no idea of what happens. The Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO) publishes a guide to the tea ceremony that explains the history, the setting and the moves a guest is expected to make. This page follows that guide, adds a practical plan for a first visit, and labels our own opinion as "Our view", so sourced information and editorial advice stay separate.

## Common mistakes

- **Booking a full formal ceremony by accident.** JNTO says a formal ceremony can last several hours. Check the length in the listing.
- **Drinking from the front of the bowl.** JNTO says to rotate the bowl slightly.
- **Worrying too much about small errors.** JNTO says minor faux pas are always overlooked.
- **Arriving in a rush.** The whole point is a slow sequence. Our view: leave a buffer before and after.

## FAQ

### What is a Japanese tea ceremony called?

JNTO says it is known as chanoyu, or sado, in Japanese, and that the art of preparing and presenting matcha is called otemae.

### How long does a tea ceremony last?

JNTO says a full, formal ceremony can last several hours, but many venues offer abbreviated versions that may be more suitable for foreign guests.

### Do you need to wear a kimono?

No. JNTO says rituals put on to demonstrate the procedures to visitors do not require formal attire.

### What if I make a mistake?

JNTO says minor faux pas are always overlooked.

### How do you drink the tea?

JNTO says to rotate the bowl slightly to avoid drinking from the front, take a sip and compliment the host on the taste and the bowl.
