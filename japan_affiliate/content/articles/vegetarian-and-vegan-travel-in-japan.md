Japan looks easy for vegetarians, with rice, vegetables, tofu and noodles on every menu. Then you read the label or ask the waiter, and discover that the noodle broth is fish stock. The Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO) has a vegetarian and vegan guide that explains the problem and the workarounds. This page summarises it and turns it into a plan you can use on day one.

Where we add advice, we label it "Our view", so sourced information and editorial opinion stay separate. Restaurants change their menus, so confirm anything important with the venue.

## A sample day, as our view

Our view: a workable day looks like this. Breakfast is a convenience store meal with fruit, a plain rice ball and a pastry that you have checked for egg and dairy. Lunch is at a restaurant you found on a portal the night before, with a photo menu so that you can point. In the afternoon you snack on edamame or roasted nuts. Dinner is the one meal worth booking in advance, with your dietary card ready. This rhythm keeps the risk to one or two meals a day, and it leaves you free to wander without stopping to decode every sign.

## FAQ

### Is it easy to be vegetarian in Japan?

JNTO says it is a challenge, but there is plenty to keep you well fed with some planning. The hidden problem is dashi and other stocks.

### How do I say I do not eat meat or fish?

Be specific: no meats, no seafood and no animal products including dashi or stocks. JNTO suggests showing a written card in Japanese.

### Are convenience stores good for vegetarians?

JNTO calls them a surprising goldmine of veggie snacks, but check fillings and sauces, since many onigiri contain fish.

### Is shojin ryori vegan?

Traditionally yes, but JNTO notes that dairy is occasionally used, so ask when you book.

### Where can I find vegetarian restaurants?

JNTO points to portal sites such as Happy Cow and Japan VegeMap, and to restaurants that display an official vegan or vegetarian sticker.
